## I. Classical Modular Forms (SL₂(ℤ) level) — What Experts Assume You Know

### 1. The q-expansion principle

**Fact:** A modular form is completely determined by its Fourier expansion at ∞. In fact, $M_k(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})) \cong \bigoplus_{n\geq 0} \mathbb{C} a_n q^n$, and the map $f \mapsto f(q)$ is an isomorphism of vector spaces onto its image. For congruence subgroups, you need Fourier expansions at all cusps, but each cusp is equivalent to ∞ under some $\alpha \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$, so $f|_k\alpha$ has a q-expansion and that's enough.

**Why experts assume it:** You never compute Petersson inner products, Hecke operators, or L-functions without writing things in terms of Fourier coefficients. The entire arithmetic content is encoded in $a_n$.

---

### 2. Valence formula (the "counting zeros" identity)

**Fact:** For a nonzero modular form $f \in M_k(\Gamma)$ with $\Gamma$ of finite index in SL₂(ℤ),
$$\frac{1}{[\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma]}\sum_{z \in \Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}} \frac{\mathrm{ord}_z(f)}{w_z} + \sum_{\text{cusps } s}\mathrm{ord}_s(f) = \frac{k}{4},$$
where $w_z$ is the order of the stabilizer of $z$ in $\Gamma/\{\pm I\}$ (so generically 1, and at elliptic points for SL₂(ℤ), $w_i=2$, $w_{\rho}=3$).

**Why experts assume it:** This is how you prove:
- $M_k(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})) = 0$ if $k < 0$ or $k$ odd (no zeros allowed)
- $\dim M_4 = \dim M_6 = 1$, so there are no modular forms of these weights for SL₂(ℤ), which forces the next possible weights
- $\dim M_{12} = 2$, spanned by Eisenstein series and cusp forms — hence $\Delta$ exists
- Any nonzero $f \in M_k$ has at least one zero in the fundamental domain (counting cusps)

---

### 3. The dimension formula for $M_k(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}))$

**Fact:** For even $k \geq 0$:
- $\dim M_0 = 1$, spanned by constants; $\dim M_2 = 0$
- For $k \geq 4$ even: $\dim M_k = \lfloor k/12 \rfloor$ if $k \not\equiv 2 \pmod{12}$, and $\lfloor k/12 \rfloor - 1$ if $k \equiv 2 \pmod{12}$

**Why experts assume it:** You need this to know when cusp forms exist ($S_k \neq 0$ iff $k \geq 12$), to understand the structure of the graded ring, and as a sanity check for everything else.

---

### 4. The graded ring $\bigoplus_{k\geq 0} M_k(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})) \cong \mathbb{C}[E_4, E_6]$

**Fact:** Every modular form for SL₂(ℤ) is a polynomial in $E_4$ and $E_6$. Equivalently, the map $\mathbb{C}[x,y]_{\text{graded}} \to \bigoplus M_k$, sending $|x|\mapsto 4$, $|y|\mapsto 6$, is an isomorphism. The cusp form ideal is generated by $\Delta = (E_4^3 - E_6^2)/1728$.

**Why experts assume it:** This tells you that the "universal" modular forms are just polynomials in two generators, and every statement about SL₂(ℤ) modular forms can be reduced to statements about $E_4$, $E_6$, and $\Delta$. The $j$-invariant is $j = E_4^3/\Delta = q^{-1} + 744 + \cdots$.

---

### 5. Hecke operators commute, are self-adjoint (w.r.t. Petersson), and generate a commutative algebra

**Fact:** For $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$, the Hecke operator $T_n$ for $n \geq 1$ is defined by
$$T_n f = n^{k-1} \sum_{ad=n,\,0\le b<d} d^{-k} z \mapsto f\left[\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & d \end{pmatrix}\right]_k.$$
Key properties experts assume:
- $T_m T_n = T_{mn}$ when $(m,n)=1$ (multiplicativity)
- $T_p^2 = T_{p^2} + p^{k-1} \langle p \rangle$ (the recurrence for prime powers; for SL₂(ℤ), $\langle p \rangle = 1$, so $T_p^2 = T_{p^2} + p^{k-1}$)
- All $T_n$ commute with each other
- Each $T_n$ is self-adjoint w.r.t. the Petersson inner product (for cusp forms)

**Why experts assume it:** This is the engine of the arithmetic theory. Eigenforms are defined by being simultaneous eigenvectors for all $T_n$. The Hecke relations give you the Euler product for L-functions: if $f$ is a normalized eigenform with $T_n f = \lambda_n f$, then $\sum a_n n^{-s} = \prod_p (1 - \lambda_p p^{-s} + p^{k-1-2s})^{-1}$.

---

### 6. The Petersson inner product and its basic properties

**Fact:** For $f,g \in S_k(\Gamma)$,
$$\langle f,g\rangle = \iint_{\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}} f(z)\overline{g(z)} y^k \frac{dx\,dy}{y^2}.$$
Experts assume: this is a positive-definite Hermitian form; the $T_n$ are self-adjoint w.r.t. it (for congruence subgroups with trivial nebentypus); and eigenforms for distinct Hecke eigenvalues are orthogonal.

---

### 7. Newform theory — Atkin-Lehner/Ulmer

**Fact:** For each level $N$, the space $S_k(\Gamma_1(N))$ decomposes into oldforms (lifted from lower levels) and newforms (primitive at every prime dividing $N$). The newform subspace is Hecke-stable, has a basis of eigenforms with algebraic integer Fourier coefficients, and each newform generates an Galois-invariant family.

**Why experts assume it:** When you talk about "the" modular form associated to something (an elliptic curve, a Galois representation), you always mean a *newform*, not just any modular form. The oldform theory explains why the same Fourier coefficients appear at multiple levels.

## II. Eisenstein Series — What Experts Assume You Know

### 8. Constant terms of Eisenstein series and their relation to ζ-functions

**Fact:** For $E_k$ on SL₂(ℤ), the constant term is $2\zeta(k)$ (unnormalized) or $1$ (normalized). For congruence subgroups, the constant term at each cusp involves ratios of zeta values. This is how you compute the Fourier expansion — it's not a miracle, it comes from the Lipschitz summation formula.

---

### 9. Real-analytic Eisenstein series $E(z,s)$ and their spectral role

**Fact:** The series $E(z,s) = \sum_{\gamma \in \Gamma_\infty\backslash\Gamma} (\Im(\gamma z))^s$ converges for $\Re(s) > 1$, has a meromorphic continuation to all of ℂ, and is an eigenfunction of the hyperbolic Laplacian with eigenvalue $s(1-s)$. The constant term involves $\xi(2s-1)y^{1-s} + \phi(s)y^s$ where $\phi(s)$ is the scattering matrix (involving ratios of ξ-functions).

**Why experts assume it:** This is how you understand the *continuous spectrum* in the spectral decomposition of $L^2(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})$. Rankin-Selberg convolutions are integrals against Eisenstein series. The functional equation for $E(z,s)$ gives the functional equation for $\zeta(s)$.

## III. Adelic Automorphic Forms — What Experts Assume You Know

### 10. The strong approximation theorem for SL₂ (and GLₙ)

**Fact:** For SL₂ over a number field F, $\mathrm{SL}_2(F)$ is dense in $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{A}_F^{\infty})$ (the finite adeles). Equivalently, any element $g \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{A}_F)$ can be written as $\gamma \cdot k_\infty \cdot k_f$ with $\gamma \in \mathrm{SL}_2(F)$, $k_\infty \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$, $k_f \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\hat{\mathcal{O}}_F)$.

**Why experts assume it:** This is how you prove that the adelic quotient $\mathrm{SL}_2(F)\backslash\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{A}_F)$ decomposes into a finite number of classical quotients — one per ideal class. For SL₂ over ℚ, there's just one double coset, so $ \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Q})\backslash\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{A}) / K_\infty K_f \cong \bigsqcup_i \Gamma_i\backslash\mathbb{H}$.

---

### 11. The Iwasawa decomposition and its adelic analogue

**Fact:** Every $g \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ can be written as $kan$ with $k \in \mathrm{SO}(2)$, $a = \begin{pmatrix} y^{1/2} & 0 \\ 0 & y^{-1/2}\end{pmatrix}$ ($y>0$), $n \in \mathbb{R}$. This identifies $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SO}(2)$ with ℍ. Experts use this constantly to translate between matrix language and z = x+iy language.

---

### 12. Satake isomorphism — the spherical Hecke algebra at unramified primes

**Fact:** For a p-adic field F and G = GLₙ(F), the spherical Hecke algebra $\mathcal{H}(G(\mathcal{O}_F) \backslash G(F) / G(\mathcal{O}_F))$ is commutative and isomorphic to a polynomial ring in n variables (the Satake parameters). For GL₂, it's $\mathbb{C}[X,X^{-1},Y,Y^{-1}]^{S_2} \cong \mathbb{C}[t_1+t_2, t_1 t_2]$.

**Why experts assume it:** This connects unramified representations to semisimple conjugacy classes in the L-group (GL₂(ℂ) = Ǧ). The Satake parameters *are* the Frobenius eigenvalues. Without this, you can't define local L-factors for automorphic representations.

---

### 13. Unramified principal series and their classification

**Fact:** For GL₂ over a p-adic field, every irreducible admissible representation that contains a nonzero fixed vector under G(𝒪) is a subquotient of an unramified principal series $\mathrm{Ind}(\chi_1 \otimes \chi_2)$ where $\chi_i$ are unramified characters. Such representations are parametrized by pairs of nonzero complex numbers (the Satake parameters).

## IV. Provable Exercises — The Ones That Cement Everything

These are statements an expert can prove in their sleep, and writing the proofs for yourself is the best way to internalize the background. Each entry lists what it uses (cross-referenced to vault contents where applicable) and why it cements things.

### Exercise A: Valence formula from argument principle
**What it uses:** Cauchy's integral theorem (`theorem:cauchy_integral_theorem`), properties of holomorphic functions on compact Riemann surfaces.
**Why it cements things:** You integrate $f'/f$ around a fundamental domain, carefully handling the elliptic points and cusps by cutting out small disks. The boundary terms give you the stabilizer weights. This is where complex analysis meets modular forms at a deep level.

---

### Exercise B: $\dim M_k(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}))$ from valence formula + induction
**What it uses:** Valence formula (from A), existence of $E_4, E_6$, the fact that $\Delta$ is a cusp form of weight 12.
**Why it cements things:** You show $M_k \cong M_{k-12}$ via multiplication by $\Delta$, giving you the dimension recurrence. This is how experts "know" the ring is $\mathbb{C}[E_4, E_6]$.

---

### Exercise C: $M_k(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})) = 0$ for odd k and k < 0
**What it uses:** Valence formula (from A) — a nonzero form of weight k must have exactly k/4 zeros counting multiplicities, which is impossible if k is odd or negative.
**Why it cements things:** This simple argument eliminates half the weights instantly and explains why modular forms are always even-weight.

---

### Exercise D: Hecke operator commutativity $T_m T_n = T_{mn}$ for (m,n)=1
**What it uses:** Double coset decomposition $\Gamma \begin{pmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & n \end{pmatrix} \Gamma = \bigsqcup \Gamma \alpha_i$, the fact that double cosets multiply: $(\Gamma\alpha\Gamma)(\Gamma\beta\Gamma) = \Gamma(\alpha\beta)\Gamma$ when (m,n)=1.
**Why it cements things:** This is the core algebraic computation behind everything. You need to understand double coset arithmetic, which is how Hecke operators are defined at every level.

---

### Exercise E: Euler product for L(f,s) from Hecke eigenvalues
**What it uses:** The Hecke recurrence $a_{mn} = a_m a_n$ for (m,n)=1 and $a_{p^r}$ relations, basic properties of Dirichlet series (`definition:dirichlet_series_of_a_sequence_of_complex_numbers`).
**Why it cements things:** This is where the arithmetic of Fourier coefficients connects to analytic properties. You prove that if $T_p f = \lambda_p p^{(k-1)/2} f$ with normalized eigenvalues, then $\sum a_n n^{-s} = \prod_p (1 - \lambda_p p^{-s} + p^{k-1-2s})^{-1}$.

---

### Exercise F: Petersson inner product makes $T_p$ self-adjoint
**What it uses:** The double coset definition of $T_p$, the invariance of the hyperbolic measure $dx\,dy/y^2$ under SL₂(ℝ), and a change-of-variables argument.
**Why it cements things:** This is where representation theory enters — self-adjointness means you get an orthogonal basis of eigenforms. Without this, the whole spectral theory collapses.

---

### Exercise G: Fourier expansion of $E_k$ via Lipschitz summation (already in vault but verify/rewrite)
**What it uses:** Lipschitz formula for $\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(z+n)^{-k}$ (`theorem:lipschitz_summation_formula_for_reciprocal_powers_on_the_upper_half_plane`), which itself follows from the partial fraction expansion of cotangent and its derivatives.
**Why it cements things:** This is where complex analysis (residue theorem applied to $\pi\cot(\pi z)$) meets modular forms. The divisor sum in the Fourier coefficients comes from reorganizing a double sum — a combinatorial argument experts do mentally.

---

### Exercise H: $E_k$ has no zeros in ℍ (for even k ≥ 4)
**What it uses:** The q-expansion of $E_k$ and the fact that its constant term dominates for large Im(z), or alternatively, valence formula + knowing where the zeros are (at elliptic points).
**Why it cements things:** This shows how to use the Fourier expansion to detect zeros — a technique you'll repeat with Hecke eigenforms and L-functions.

---

### Exercise I: The j-invariant gives an isomorphism $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H} \cong \mathbb{C}$
**What it uses:** $j = E_4^3/\Delta$, the valence formula to show j has a simple pole at ∞, and basic Riemann surface theory.
**Why it cements things:** This is where modular forms become *functions on moduli spaces*. The j-invariant classifies elliptic curves up to isomorphism over ℂ.

---

### Exercise J: Mellin transform of $f(iy)$ = completed L-function; analytic continuation + functional equation (extend the one already in content.tex)
**What it uses:** The integral representation already written, plus the modular transformation $f(-1/z) = z^k f(z)$ to prove analytic continuation and functional equation.
**Why it cements things:** This is the prototype for all L-function proofs. You split $\int_0^\infty$ into $\int_0^1 + \int_1^\infty$, use $f(i/y) = y^k f(iy)$ to change variables in the first integral, and get the functional equation from the Gamma factor symmetry $\Gamma(s) \to \Gamma(k-s)$.

---

### Exercise K: Strong approximation for SL₂(ℚ)
**What it uses:** Chinese remainder theorem, Dirichlet's approximation theorem (or continued fractions), and the definition of adeles.
**Why it cements things:** This is how you prove that classical modular forms for Γ(N) correspond to adelic automorphic forms with level $K = \ker(\mathrm{SL}_2(\hat{\mathbb{Z}}) \to \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}))$. Without strong approximation, the bridge between classical and adelic language breaks.

---

### Exercise L: Satake isomorphism for GL₂(ℚₚ)
**What it uses:** Iwasawa decomposition G = BK, Bruhat decomposition G = B ⊔ BN, and explicit computation of double coset multiplication.
**Why it cements things:** This connects the Hecke algebra to the L-group. The isomorphism sends a spherical function φ to a Weyl-invariant Laurent polynomial — for GL₂, the two Satake parameters α, β satisfy $\phi(\mathrm{diag}(p,1)) = \alpha + \beta$ and $\phi(\mathrm{diag}(p,p)) = 1 + \alpha\beta$.

---

### Exercise M: Rankin-Selberg convolution as a Mellin transform
**What it uses:** The integral representation $\int_0^\infty \langle f|_{k} y^{k/2}, g\r q(y) \frac{dy}{y}$, unfolding the Eisenstein series, and comparing with Dirichlet series.
**Why it cements things:** This is where you prove analytic continuation and functional equation for $L(f\otimes g,s)$ by "unfolding" — one of the most powerful techniques in automorphic forms.

---

### Exercise N: Deligne's bound |a_p| ≤ 2p^{(k-1)/2} from Weil conjectures
**What it uses:** Eichler-Shimura (construction of elliptic curve with good reduction at p, Frobenius trace = a_p), Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields.
**Why it cements things:** This is where geometry (Weil II) gives the Ramanujan-Petersson conjecture for classical modular forms. It's also the prototype for all modern bounds on L-function coefficients.
