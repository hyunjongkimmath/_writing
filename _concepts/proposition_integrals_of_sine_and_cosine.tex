\begin{proposition} \label{proposition:integrals_of_sine_and_cosine}

For any $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$,
$$\hlin{\int_a^b \sin(x) \, dx = \cos(a) - \cos(b)}$$
$$\hlin{\int_a^b \cos(x) \, dx = \sin(b) - \sin(a)}$$
where the integrals are \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:lower_upper_riemann_sum_integral_of_a_bounded_function_on_a_closed_interval_to_R_and_riemann_integrable_function}{Riemann integrals}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}

    The sine and cosine functions are differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$ with derivatives (see \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{proposition:derivatives_of_sine_and_cosine}):
    $$\frac{d}{dx} \sin(x) = \cos(x), \qquad \frac{d}{dx} \cos(x) = -\sin(x).$$

    Since they are differentiable, they are continuous (by \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{proposition:differentiability_at_a_point_for_a_function_on_an_interval_of_the_real_numbers_valued_in_the_real_numbers_implies_continuity_at_a_point}), and therefore Riemann integrable on any closed bounded interval $[a, b]$ by \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{proposition:continuous_functions_are_riemann_integrable_on_closed_intervals}.

    We observe that $\cos(x)$ is an antiderivative of $\sin(x)$ up to sign. Specifically, the function $x \mapsto -\cos(x)$ has derivative
    $$\frac{d}{dx}(-\cos(x)) = -(-\sin(x)) = \sin(x).$$
    \CrefIfExists{proposition:derivatives_of_sine_and_cosine}\CrefIfExists{corollary:derivative_of_real_scalar_multiple_of_real_valued_differentiable_function_on_real_interval_at_a_point_is_scalar_multiple_of_derivative}

    Similarly, $\sin(x)$ is an antiderivative of $\cos(x)$ since
    $$\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x) = \cos(x).$$

    Applying the \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{theorem:fundamental_theorem_of_calculus_part_two_evaluation_by_antiderivative}{Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part II}:
    $$\int_a^b \sin(x)\,dx = \Bigl[-\cos(x)\Bigr]_{x=a}^{x=b} = -\cos(b) + \cos(a) = \cos(a) - \cos(b),$$
    and
    $$\int_a^b \cos(x)\,dx = \Bigl[\sin(x)\Bigr]_{x=a}^{x=b} = \sin(b) - \sin(a).$$
\end{proof}
