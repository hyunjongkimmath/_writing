\begin{theorem} \label{theorem:eulers_formula_for_complex_exponential}
[Euler's Formula] For every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, the complex exponential satisfies
$$\hlin{\exp(ix) = \cos(x) + i\sin(x)}$$
where $\exp$ is the \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:complex_exponential_function}{complex extension of} the \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:real_exponential_function}{exponential function}, and $\sin$, $\cos$ are the \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:sine_and_cosine_functions_on_real_line_via_power_series}{sine and cosine functions}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    We compare the power series expansions of both sides. The complex exponential is defined by (see \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:complex_exponential_function}):
    $$\exp(z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{z^n}{n!},$$
    which converges absolutely for every $z \in \mathbb{C}$. Substituting $z = ix$:
    $$\exp(ix) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(ix)^n}{n!}.$$

    We separate the terms of this series into even and odd indices. For each nonnegative integer $k$, we have:
    $$i^{2k} = (i^2)^k = (-1)^k, \qquad i^{2k+1} = i^{2k} \cdot i = (-1)^k i.$$

    Splitting the sum accordingly:
    $$\exp(ix) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{i^{2k} x^{2k}}{(2k)!} + \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{i^{2k+1} x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}$$
    $$= \sum_{k=0}^\infty (-1)^k \frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!} + i \sum_{k=0}^\infty (-1)^k \frac{x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}.$$

    The first series is the power series for $\cos(x)$ and the second is the power series for $\sin(x)$\CrefIfExists{definition:sine_and_cosine_functions_on_real_line_via_power_series}:
    $$\exp(ix) = \cos(x) + i\sin(x).$$
\end{proof}
