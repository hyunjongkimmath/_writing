Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ and $(Y, \mathcal{B}, \nu)$ be two \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:measure_on_a_measurable_space_and_measure_space}{$\sigma$-finite measure spaces}. Let $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$ be the \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:product_sigma_algebra_and_product_measure_for_two_measure_spaces}{product $\sigma$-algebra} and $\mu \times \nu$ the corresponding \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:product_sigma_algebra_and_product_measure_for_two_measure_spaces}{product measure}.

Let $f : X \times Y \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$ be a \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:measurable_function_between_measure_spaces}{measurable function} with respect to $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$ such that $f$ is \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:lebesgue_integral_of_measurable_functions_into_the_real_line_extended_by_infinities}{Lebesgue integrable}, i.e.
$$\int_{X \times Y} |f|\,d(\mu \times \nu) < \infty.$$

Then for $\mu$-almost every $x \in X$, the function $y \mapsto f(x,y)$ is $\nu$-integrable on $Y$, and the function
$$x \mapsto \int_Y f(x,y)\,d\nu(y)$$
is $\mu$-integrable on $X$. Similarly, for $\nu$-almost every $y \in Y$, the function $x \mapsto f(x,y)$ is $\mu$-integrable on $X$, and
$$\int_X \left(\int_Y f(x,y)\,d\nu(y)\right) d\mu(x) = \int_{X \times Y} f\,d(\mu \times \nu) = \int_Y \left(\int_X f(x,y)\,d\mu(x)\right) d\nu(y).$$
\end{theorem}

    Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ and $(Y, \mathcal{B}, \nu)$ be $\sigma$-finite measure spaces. Fix the integrable function $f : X \times Y \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$.

    Since $|f|$ is nonnegative and measurable, by Tonelli's theorem (\CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{theorem:tonelli_theorem}) applied to $|f|$:
    $$\int_X \left(\int_Y |f(x,y)|\,d\nu(y)\right) d\mu(x) = \int_{X \times Y} |f|\,d(\mu \times \nu) < \infty.$$

    Because the iterated integral of $|f|$ is finite, for $\mu$-almost every $x \in X$, the inner integral $\int_Y |f(x,y)|\,d\nu(y)$ must be finite. For such $x$, the function $y \mapsto f(x,y)$ is therefore $\nu$-integrable. This establishes that the map
    $$x \mapsto \int_Y f(x,y)\,d\nu(y)$$
    is defined for almost every $x$.

    Moreover, this function is measurable (as a consequence of the measurability of $f$ and Fubini-Tonelli theory). To see that it is integrable:
    $$\int_X \left|\int_Y f(x,y)\,d\nu(y)\right| d\mu(x) \leq \int_X \left(\int_Y |f(x,y)|\,d\nu(y)\right) d\mu(x) = \int_{X \times Y} |f|\,d(\mu \times \nu) < \infty.$$

    Now decompose $f$ into its positive and negative parts: $f = f_+ - f_-$. Both $f_+$ and $f_-$ are nonnegative measurable functions. By Tonelli's theorem applied to each:
    $$\int_{X \times Y} f_+\,d(\mu \times \nu) = \int_X \left(\int_Y f_+(x,y)\,d\nu(y)\right) d\mu(x),$$
    and similarly for $f_-$. Since both integrals are finite (because $|f|$ is integrable), we can subtract:
    $$\int_{X \times Y} f\,d(\mu \times \nu) = \int_{X \times Y} f_+\,d(\mu \times \nu) - \int_{X \times Y} f_-\,d(\mu \times \nu).$$

    By the linearity of the Lebesgue integral (\CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{theorem:linearity_of_lebesgue_integral}):
    $$\int_X \left(\int_Y f(x,y)\,d\nu(y)\right) d\mu(x) = \int_{X \times Y} f\,d(\mu \times \nu).$$

    The same argument with the order of integration reversed gives:
    $$\int_Y \left(\int_X f(x,y)\,d\mu(x)\right) d\nu(y) = \int_{X \times Y} f\,d(\mu \times \nu).$$

    Combining these equalities establishes Fubini's theorem.
\end{proof}
