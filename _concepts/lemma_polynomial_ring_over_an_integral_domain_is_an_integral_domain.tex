\begin{lemma} \label{lemma:polynomial_ring_over_an_integral_domain_is_an_integral_domain}
    Let $R$ be an \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:domain_integral_domain}{integral domain}.
    For any integer $n \geq 1$, the \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:polynomial_ring_over_a_commutative_ring}{polynomial ring} $R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is an integral domain.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    We first show that $R[x]$ is an integral domain. Let $f, g \in R[x]$ be nonzero polynomials. Write
    $$f = a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_d x^d \quad \text{and} \quad g = b_0 + b_1 x + \cdots + b_e x^e,$$
    where $a_d \neq 0$ and $b_e \neq 0$ are the \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:degree_and_leading_coefficient_of_a_polynomial}{leading coefficients} of $f$ and $g$, respectively, with $d = \deg(f)$\CrefIfExists{definition:degree_and_leading_coefficient_of_a_polynomial} and $e = \deg(g)$.

    The product $fg$ has as its coefficient of $x^{d+e}$ the element $a_d b_e$. Since $R$ is an \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:domain_integral_domain}{integral domain}, it has no nontrivial zero divisors, so $a_d b_e \neq 0$. Hence the coefficient of $x^{d+e}$ in $fg$ is nonzero, which implies that $fg \neq 0$. Therefore, $R[x]$ has no nontrivial zero divisors and is an integral domain.

    For the general case with $n$ variables, observe that $R[x_1,\ldots,x_n] = (R[x_1,\ldots,x_{n-1}])[x_n]$. By induction on $n$, if $R[x_1,\ldots,x_{n-1}]$ is an integral domain, then adjoining the variable $x_n$ yields another integral domain by the single-variable case established above. The base case $n=1$ is exactly the argument above.

    In all cases, $R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is an \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:domain_integral_domain}{integral domain}.
\end{proof}
