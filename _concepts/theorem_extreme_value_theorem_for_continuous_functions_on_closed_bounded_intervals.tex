\begin{theorem}[Extreme value theorem for continuous functions on closed bounded intervals] \label{theorem:extreme_value_theorem_for_continuous_functions_on_closed_bounded_intervals}
    Let $[a, b] \subseteq \mathbb{R}$\CrefIfExists{definition:field_of_real_numbers} be a closed bounded interval with $a < b$, and let $f : [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be a \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:function_of_sets}{function}. Equip $\mathbb{R}$ with the \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:standard_absolute_value_on_real_numbers}{standard topology} induced by the Euclidean metric, and equip $[a, b]$ with the subspace topology.

    If $f$ is \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:continuous_map_of_topological_spaces}{continuous}, then there exist $c, d \in [a, b]$ such that
    $$f(c) = \inf_{x \in [a,b]} f(x) \quad \text{and} \quad f(d) = \sup_{x \in [a,b]} f(x).$$
    In other words, $f$ attains both its \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:least_element_in_a_subset_of_a_partially_ordered_set}{minimum and maximum} on $[a, b]$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    We prove that $f$ attains its maximum; the minimum follows by applying the same argument to $-f$. Let $M = \sup_{x \in [a,b]} f(x) \in \mathbb{R} \cup \{+\infty\}$.

    First, we show that $M < \infty$, i.e., $f$ is \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:bounded_above_below_and_bounded_function_on_a_set_to_an_ordered_set}{bounded above}. Suppose for contradiction that $f$ is not bounded above. Then there exists a sequence $(x_n)$ in $[a,b]$ such that $f(x_n) > n$ for each $n$. By \Cref{theorem:bolzano_weierstrass_theorem_stating_that_bounded_sequences_in_real_numbers_have_subsequences_that_converge_to_some_value}, the bounded sequence $(x_n)$ has a \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:convergence_and_limit_of_sequence_in_extended_metric_space_valued_in_ordered_commutative_monoid_with_adjoined_infinity}{convergent subsequence} $(x_{n_k})$ with limit $x^* \in [a,b]$. Since $f$ is continuous at $x^*$, we have $\lim_{k \to \infty} f(x_{n_k}) = f(x^*) \in \mathbb{R}$\CrefIfExists{proposition:sequential_characterization_of_continuity_at_a_point_for_functions_between_extended_metric_space_valued_in_ordered_commutative_monoids_with_adjoined_infinity}, which contradicts that $f(x_{n_k}) > n_k \to +\infty$. Therefore, $f$ is bounded above, and $M < \infty$.

    Now we show that the supremum $M$ is attained. By definition of supremum, there exists a sequence $(y_n)$ in $[a,b]$ such that $f(y_n) > M - \frac{1}{n}$ for each $n$. Again by \Cref{theorem:bolzano_weierstrass_theorem_stating_that_bounded_sequences_in_real_numbers_have_subsequences_that_converge_to_some_value}, the bounded sequence $(y_n)$ has a convergent subsequence $(y_{n_k})$ with limit $d \in [a,b]$. By continuity of $f$, we have $\lim_{k \to \infty} f(y_{n_k}) = f(d)$ by \Cref{proposition:sequential_characterization_of_continuity_at_a_point_for_functions_between_extended_metric_space_valued_in_ordered_commutative_monoids_with_adjoined_infinity}. But for each $k$,
    $$M \geq f(y_{n_k}) > M - \frac{1}{n_k},$$
    so taking limits as $k \to \infty$ yields $f(d) = M$ by the \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{theorem:squeeze_theorem_for_limits_of_real_valued_functions_on_real_numbers}{squeeze theorem}. Therefore, $f$ attains its maximum at $d \in [a,b]$.
\end{proof}
