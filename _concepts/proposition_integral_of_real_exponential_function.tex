\begin{proposition} \label{proposition:integral_of_real_exponential_function}
For any $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $c \neq 0$,
$$\hlin{\int_a^b e^{cx} \, dx = \frac{1}{c}\left(e^{cb} - e^{ca}\right)}$$
In particular, $\int_0^x e^t \, dt = e^x - 1$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, where the integral is the \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:lower_upper_riemann_sum_integral_of_a_bounded_function_on_a_closed_interval_to_R_and_riemann_integrable_function}{Riemann integral}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Recall that $\exp(x) = e^x$ is \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:derivative_of_a_real_valued_function_on_a_real_interval}{differentiable} on $\bbR$\CrefIfExists{proposition:derivative_of_exponential_function_is_itself} and has derivative $\exp(x)$. By the \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{proposition:sum_rule_product_rule_quotient_rule_chain_rule_for_real_derivatives_of_real_valued_functions_on_real_intervals}{chain rule}, we have 
    $$\frac{d}{dx} e^{cx} = \frac{d}{dy} e^{y} \cdot \frac{d}{dx} (cx) = e^y \cdot c = c \cdot e^{cx}.$$
    Therefore, 
    $$\frac{d}{dx} \frac{1}{c} e^{cx} = e^{cx}.$$
    \CrefIfExists{theorem:linearity_of_riemann_integral}
    In particular, $e^{cx}$ is differentiable on $\bbR$ as well. Moreover, $e^{cx}$ is continuous on $\bbR$, and hence is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$\CrefIfExists{proposition:continuous_functions_are_riemann_integrable_on_closed_intervals}. Therefore, by the \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{theorem:fundamental_theorem_of_calculus_part_two_evaluation_by_antiderivative}{fundamental theorem of calculus}, 
    $$\int_a^b e^{cx} dx = \frac{1}{c} e^{cx} \biggr \vert_a^b = \frac{e^{cb} - e^{ca}}{c}$$
    as desired. 
\end{proof}
