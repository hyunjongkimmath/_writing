\begin{theorem}[Smooth function with vanishing Taylor series] \label{theorem:example_of_a_smooth_function_with_vanishing_taylor_series_not_equal_to_zero}

    There exist infinitely differentiable functions $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ whose Taylor series at a point converges but does not equal the function. Specifically, define
    $$f(x) := \begin{cases} e^{-1/x^2} & \text{if } x \neq 0, \\ 0 & \text{if } x = 0. \end{cases}$$
    Then $f$ is infinitely differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$, and for every integer $k \geq 0$, the $k$th derivative at zero satisfies $f^{(k)}(0) = 0$. Consequently, the Taylor series of $f$ centered at $0$ is the identically zero power series, which converges everywhere to $0$, but $f(x) > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$. In particular, $f$ is not \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:real_analytic_function_on_an_open_interval}{real analytic at $0$}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}

We show that $f^{(k)}(0) = 0$ for all $k \geq 0$ by induction. The function is clearly smooth on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$ as a composition of smooth functions away from zero.

First, we establish the base case $k = 0$. At $x = 0$, we have $f(0) = 0$ by definition. To show that $f$ is differentiable at $0$, we compute the difference quotient:
$$\frac{f(x) - f(0)}{x} = \frac{e^{-1/x^2}}{x},$$
for $x \neq 0$. We show that this tends to zero as $x \to 0$. Substituting $t = 1/|x|$ so that $t \to \infty$ as $x \to 0$, we have
$$\left|\frac{e^{-1/x^2}}{x}\right| = t e^{-t}.$$
Since $e^{t} \geq t^3 / 6$ for all sufficiently large $t$, we obtain $t e^{-t} \leq 6/t^2 \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$. Therefore, $\lim_{x \to 0} (f(x) - f(0))/x = 0$, so $f'(0) = 0$.

Now we proceed by induction. Suppose that for some $n \geq 1$, we have $f^{(k)}(0) = 0$ for all $k = 0, 1, \dots, n$, and suppose that for $x \neq 0$, the $n$-th derivative has the form
$$f^{(n)}(x) = P_n(1/x)e^{-1/x^2},$$
where $P_n$ is a polynomial. We show that $f^{(n+1)}(0) = 0$. The difference quotient at $0$ is
$$\frac{f^{(n)}(x) - f^{(n)}(0)}{x} = \frac{P_n(1/x)e^{-1/x^2}}{x}.$$
Setting $t = 1/|x|$, the expression becomes
$$\left|\frac{P_n(t)e^{-t^2}}{1/t}\right| = t |P_n(t)| e^{-t^2}.$$
For any polynomial $Q(t)$, we have $\lim_{t \to \infty} Q(t) e^{-t^2} = 0$ because the exponential decay dominates polynomial growth. Therefore, the difference quotient tends to zero as $x \to 0$, so $f^{(n+1)}(0) = 0$.

We verify that the derivative has the correct form for $x \neq 0$. Differentiating $P_n(1/x)e^{-1/x^2}$ using the product rule and chain rule gives
$$\frac{d}{dx} P_n(1/x)e^{-1/x^2} = -\frac{P_n'(1/x)}{x^2}e^{-1/x^2} + P_n(1/x) \cdot \frac{2}{x^3} e^{-1/x^2},$$
which can be written as $P_{n+1}(1/x)e^{-1/x^2}$ for a polynomial $P_{n+1}$. This completes the inductive step.

We conclude that $f^{(k)}(0) = 0$ for all $k \geq 0$. The Taylor series of $f$ centered at $0$ is therefore
$$\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!} x^k = \sum_{k=0}^\infty 0 \cdot x^k = 0.$$
This series converges everywhere to the zero function. However, $f(x) = e^{-1/x^2} > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$. Consequently, $f$ does not equal its Taylor series in any neighborhood of $0$, so $f$ is not real analytic at $0$.
\end{proof}
