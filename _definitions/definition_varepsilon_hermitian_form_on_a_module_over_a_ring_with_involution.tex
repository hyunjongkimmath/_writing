\begin{definition} \label{definition:varepsilon_hermitian_form_on_a_module_over_a_ring_with_involution}
Let $(A, \sigma)$ be a \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:involution_on_a_ring}{ring with involution} where $\sigma$ is also denoted by $\overline{\cdot}$, let $\varepsilon \in Z(A)$ be an element such that $\varepsilon \cdot \overline{\varepsilon} = 1$, and let $M$ be a \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:module_of_a_ring}{(left or right) $A$-module}.
A \hldef{$\varepsilon$-hermitian form on $M$} (or simply \hldef{hermitian form on $M$}, especially when $\varepsilon$ is understood or is just $1$) is a \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:sesquilinear_form_on_a_module_over_a_ring_with_involution}{$\sigma$-sesquilinear form} $\phi: M \times M \to A$ satisfying the symmetry condition:
$$ \phi(y, x) = \varepsilon \cdot \overline{\phi(x, y)} $$
for all $x, y \in M$. The pair $(M, \phi)$ is often called a \hldef{hermitian module}.

$1$-hermitian forms are may simply be called \hldef{hermitian forms}, and $(-1)$-hermitian forms are called \hldef{skew-hermitian forms}.

As with \CrefAndHyperrefIfExist{definition:sesquilinear_form_on_a_module_over_a_ring_with_involution}{sesquilinear forms} more generally, $\varepsilon$-Hermitian forms are either conjugate linear on the first variable and linear on the second variable or linear on the first variable and conjugate linear on the second variable, depending on whether $M$ is regarded as a right $A$-module or a left $A$-module.
\end{definition}
